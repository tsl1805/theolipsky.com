The Error-Besotten Angel Down

Venue: The Republic of Letters

Dated: September 23, 2026

By: Theo Lipsky

A plunge into the heart of World War I; that is what the book jacket of Daniel Kraus’ Angel Down promises. Open the enclosed novel and one meets Cyril Bagger, an aspiring nihilist and confidence man, drafted into service with the American Expeditionary Force. Bagger has managed to duck combat by way of a gravedigging detail. He spends his off-duty hours fleecing fellow soldiers in rigged games of cards. In this way Cyril Bagger embodies the conceit of the novel, for Angel Down is one big bluff. That the book just won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction suggests that as a bluff it succeeded. Still, I would like to try to call that bluff now.

Angel Down is a bluff because it does not plunge into the heart of World War I. In fact, Angel Down has very little to do with World War I at all. Its indifference to the war is suggested not only by the errors which dog the book from start to finish but by the book’s cartoonish and hyperviolent style. On their own, neither would compromise a novel. But such indifference to the war matters, as does its style, because the novel has a distinct moral project. That’s where World War I comes in.

Just as any bluff takes its strength from the imagined hand behind it, Angel Down takes its moral force from the bleak spectacle of World War I, that canonical Armageddon. For this reason it must achieve a verisimilitude to the Great War in the minds of its reader. To that end, Daniel Kraus, a proven talent, laces endless references and proper nouns into his breathless narration. These he intends to give the texture of a bedraggled doughboy’s life on the front, all in the attempted voice of some turn-of-the-century Iowan. Neither succeed in creating that verisimilitude, for reasons we’ll see.

Errors of Fact

Let’s consider Angel Down’s errors of fact first. Ought we care? The novel is a work of magical realism. It is not a documentary. The event to which its title alludes ought to make this plain enough: we meet Bagger just before he discovers in no-man’s land an actual angel fallen from heaven, complete with beams of heavenly light. Bagger then carries his angel on a hellish trip from the front lines to his higher headquarters in hopes of saving her. Who cares if the book takes some more mundane liberties along the way?

The trouble is that among Angel Down’s endless world-building references, meant to craft that verisimilitude against which the appearance of an angel so compels us, are simply too many errors. What follows is a short inventory of these errors. It is intended only to demonstrate that they are characteristic, not exceptional:

  • Bagger is relieved early that “he’s not gone 4-F, not yet” a full 22 years before the 4-F category of draft deferment came to exist.
  • Bagger worries that his M1903 Springfield Rifle will explode because “cartridges bloat in wet weather” but the M1903 ammunition did not bloat as it was brass. Such problems ended in the mid-19th century.
  • Bagger watches a hasty retreat during which “men toss other men Springfields and Enfields and Garands” but the first M1 Garand rifle would not be fielded until 1936, 18 years after the Meuse-Argonne Offensive, during which the eponymous John Garand was still an unknown machinist in New York.
  • Bagger, in a passage typical of the irreverent and hard-nosed voice Kraus cultivates, thinks to himself that “he needs to get these fractious fucks out of Dodge” but the phrase “get out of Dodge” almost certainly entered the American lexicon via the television show Gunsmoke, in the 1950s.
  • Bagger describes the rubble of no-man’s land “as if German Fokkers have taken to dropping brick in lieu of bombs” but no Fokker aircraft functioned as bombers during World War One. They were fighters.
  • Bagger at one point watches a passing German column and marvels that “they aren’t even collecting the German dead, only the dog tags” but the first English record of the term “dog tags” comes courtesy of William Randolph Hearst in 1936.
  • Bagger is said to have learned his card-cheat ways back home, where he was “banned from half the riverboats on the Mississippi,” but the legendary gambling culture aboard riverboats on the Mississippi died out decades before Bagger could’ve been born.
  • Bagger at one point remembers his clergy father gifting him a Bible “the way Red Cross nurses give a man about to be amputated a rag to bite on,” but by World War I amputation patients received anesthesia even at the forward-most field hospitals, even during the Meuse-Argonne offensive, even a half-century before in the American Civil War.

What explains these errors? They do not advance the novel’s magical realism. They are not plausible misperceptions of the protagonist, for how can he mistakenly name a rifle that does not exist? For an explanation, one would have to ask the author. Last July an interviewer from the Chicago Review of Books effectively did that, if semi-wittingly, when she queried Daniel Kraus about his research process for Angel Down:

Interviewer: Well, the setting itself is so visceral and gruesomely alive. What was your research process?

Daniel Kraus: I have to be aware not to do too much research because I’ve gotten lost in research before. And particularly if you’re dealing with war, there’s just an endless amount of sources to deal with. Everything’s a gigantic can of worms. But research for this book was relatively straightforward, as opposed to my previous novel, Whalefall, for example, where I had to do a lot of in-person interviews. I was basically dealing with books and documentaries and second-hand materials. My theory on research is that the reader doesn’t have to follow it all. If I’m running through various details about machine guns, for example, the reader doesn’t have to necessarily understand what I’m talking about. They just have to believe that I know what I’m talking about.

Put another way, Kraus refrained from doing “too much research” and bet the reader would think he had. The interviewer’s very next question is about Bagger the con man. Still, neither she nor Kraus draws the self-evident parallel: both the protagonist and author of Angel Down ask for customers’ confidence though they don’t deliver the underlying good. This does not indict Kraus’ character — all artists manipulate us, we know as much, and still, we submit to their art — but it does give us a simple explanation for the errors that plague the novel. To Kraus’ credit, his bet that readers would believe that he knew what he was talking about seems to have paid off. In commercial terms, Angel Down succeeded.

Excess of Style

If Angel Down is a con, what does it con from the reader? What harm is done by some mistakes about equipment that only a nerd would notice?

To know, we must return to the matter of Kraus’ style. Search the witness accounts of World War I. You will find few if any, even among the frankest and least sentimental, that tell of battlefield violence the way Kraus chooses to. Remarque may mention men without mouths or else intestines spilling out, but he does so in clipped and grim fashion. Graves may tell you what a trench suicide looks like, but in a matter-of-fact accent. Blunden tells you of an eyeball under a duckboard, but clearly to relieve his soul, if only for a moment, on a page.

Angel Down, in turn, treats the violence of the First World War in an almost gluttonous way. Not a page in, Bagger meets a corpse “stomped so flat by a shell that his spinal column protrudes from his gaping mouth” (that an explosive shell might achieve such a fragile sculptural effect seems less likely than that Kraus worked backwards from his imagination to the war). This would bother Bagger but he’s developed “a sommelier palate for the tart fizz of brachial blood, the fudgy sorghum of femoral, the meaty sludge of heart wounds, the rancid reek of any gut juice at all,” and so on. He shortly stumbles upon a “scalloped” crater so bloody it has “something yuletide about it, the color of fruitcake.”

The culinary metaphors persist well past the first pages. A shell cuts a soldier in half “neat as a holiday fruitcake” (fruitcakes return several times in the book). The halved soldiers’ legs tip forward, “the pelvic bowl pouring steaming intestines into the trench.” The blood of a murdered soldier leaves his olive drabs “watermelon striped.” The hole left by a destroyed nose bears “the precise contours of a succulent pear.” A foe’s grimace “fatted into rolls of arterial sausage.” One learns of an “eggy epidermis,” later of some hair “raspberry with blood.” When food is finally exhausted of its metaphorical potential, the narrator turns to other appetites. Semen is enlisted at least three times as a simile.

The only other principle that disciplines the novel’s style is that same principle which governed the war the novel derides: mass. Kraus’ prose, a single much-jointed sentence, deploys wave after wave of clever formulations at the reader to convey the force of the war. The best of these formulations suggest Daniel Kraus’ gift for the surreal image. The land behind the front “goes dreamy,” talking mouths “drawbridge down,” a novel tank even “galumphs” past. He likewise has an ear for the musical and a sense for his reader’s expectations, expectations he’s seeking always to subvert with some new analogy for a mutilation, material or spiritual. Just when you’ve made sense of it, he sends another metaphor over the top at you.

But like too many of the troops they mimic, Kraus’ words waves are largely wasted. Wasted because this unrelenting cartoon hyperviolence undermines its purpose. Such language might have been alien to the likes of Graves or Remarque but in today’s reader it lights a flash of recognition. We know these images as schlock. Rendered with art, they are Tarantino fare. On the screen such cartoon violence is palatable for being pure entertainment. Even the most sincere viewer of Inglourious Basterds knows he is watching something with barely a connection to the Holocaust. These connections serve only to license the indulgence of our violent fantasies. We feel ourselves free to cheer mutilation because it is a Nazi being mutilated. Beyond that, World War II serves the film mostly as a costume closet.

The Spiritualism Con

What makes the novel a con is that, unlike the schlock it recalls, the novel does have a moral project. Its end makes this all too plain. In the final pages, the rescued angel takes Bagger on a revelatory trip through a hole in the sky to his father’s church, then down through the collapsing floorboards to the center of the earth, all the while asking him questions such as “What does it matter which nations win which wars?” At the earth’s core Bagger beholds a great cosmic machine that turns the flesh of the war’s dead into fresh bullets. “Genesis incorrect after all,” the narrator intones, “life doesn’t beget life, it’s death that begets death, so foundational a principle it has become civilization’s engine, an inferno of grief…a cycle perfected by the so-called War to End All Wars, which, Bagger understands with horror, is really the War to Begin All Wars,” and so on.

The angel shows Bagger this death factory at earth’s core to show him how the moral universe in fact works. One gets the sense that’s what Kraus also thinks he’s doing for the reader in the 250 pages of grotesqueries that precede the book’s climax. Far from profaning the war’s dead and maimed, Kraus’ thinking might’ve gone, the novel’s hyperviolent mode honors them by confronting the reader with an unflinching look at the war’s foul cost. Likely Daniel Kraus had these good intentions.

The issue is that Angel Down does flinch, over and over, in repeatedly failing to get right the fundamental facts of the war which gave shape to the human experience of fighting it. How else are we to read the author’s explanation for his own disinclination to research the war but as one great flinch? So too does the novel’s hyperviolence function as a flinch away from underlying, duller horrors. Of course the novel ends up in an angel’s voice suggesting that all wars are the same — the novel doesn’t take enough interest in the facts of wars to distinguish one from another. That the Pulitzer Board would likewise fail only suggests how incurious we’ve gotten about the wars that still shape our moral imagination.

Before taking a final account of this ambitious endgame, consider one more objection: perhaps all this might be better understood if one recalls Kraus’ background as a writer of horror, of science fiction, of young adult novels. Might that reframe the cartoons which animate the book? No. Genre fiction don’t foreclose a substantive engagement with the First World War as a historical fact. Far from it. Perhaps the greatest work to emerge from that war belonged to all those genres and more. It was J.R.R. Tolkien’s Lord of the Rings. In his account of the war’s effect on Tolkien, John Garth quotes another veteran writer complaining about writers who wrote strictly “prose of horror” about the war: “The sight of blood has gone to their heads. They can see nothing else…” Tolkien, Garth suggests, stood triumphantly against such dead-end disenchantment.

So Angel Down’s closing moral intonations ring needlessly false after all its ahistorical stumbles and cartoonish violence. The novel’s descent into earth’s core serves only to highlight how extractive the whole thing is. Rather than attend to the actual war which turns the wheels of the imagined Armageddon machine, rather than dignify the violence of the war with something short of gluttony, the novel clambers into the First World War only far enough and narrowly enough to mine the war for its moral heft. It then returns to the surface, calls itself a war novel, and bets that its readers, covetous of the moral force history promises but averse to any “can of worms,” won’t care enough to notice. After all, as Bagger declares early, “the spiritualism con is a growth area.” But we ought to take notice, so let’s call the bluff.
